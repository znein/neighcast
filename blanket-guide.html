The blanket guide

When to blanket, and which weight

Neighcast uses this chart. Temperatures are the coldest "feels like" temperature overnight, which includes wind chill.

The clipped column runs about 5°F warmer than many classic charts, in line with Rutgers (sheet clipped horses below 60°F) and with what owners in mild climates actually use. Fill weights: sheet = no fill; lightweight ≈ 50–150g; mediumweight ≈ 180–250g; heavyweight = 300g+.

What changes the call

  • Rain, sleet or wet snow. A wet coat lies flat and stops insulating. A horse in a full coat can usually handle the cold but not cold and wet, so a waterproof sheet keeps the coat dry and fluffy. Always use a waterproof turnout in wet weather.
  • Wind. Wind strips away warmth, so Neighcast uses the wind-chill ("feels like") temperature. Strong gusts with no shelter bump the call up a weight.
  • Shelter. A run-in shed takes much of the wind's bite away, so Neighcast counts only about half the wind chill. A box stall inside a barn takes the wind and the wet away entirely, so Neighcast uses the plain air temperature, and a stable blanket is fine instead of a waterproof turnout.
  • The horse. Seniors, thin horses, hard keepers and horses not used to the cold need one weight more. Fit, well-fed horses with a thick coat often need less than the chart says.
  • Hay. Digesting forage makes heat. On the coldest nights, extra hay is the best blanket there is.

Check you got it right

  • Slide a hand under the blanket at the withers and behind the shoulder. Warm and dry is right. Damp or sweaty means too much blanket: a sweaty horse gets chilled.
  • Shivering, a tucked-up tail or standing hunched with the back to the wind means too little.
  • Watch the daytime high. A blanket that's right at 20°F overnight is far too warm at 50°F at noon. Neighcast warns you when to take it off or swap down.
